📘 Algebra: Complete Learning Chapter

Work through each section in order. Follow the arrows, study the examples, then complete the practice quiz.

What is algebra?

Algebra is the branch of mathematics that uses letters and symbols to represent numbers and relationships.

An unknown number → represent it with x
↓
Describe the relationship → form an equation
↓
Solve the equation → find the unknown value

1. Algebraic expressions and like terms

An algebraic expression is a mathematical phrase containing numbers, variables and operations.

Example: 3x + 5

Variable → a letter representing a number.
Coefficient → the number multiplying a variable.
Constant → a number without a variable.
Term → a part separated by plus or minus signs.

An expression can contain several terms.

Simplifying like terms

Like terms have the same variables raised to the same powers. Combine their coefficients, but do not combine unlike terms.

4x + 3x − 2 + 5
↓
Group like terms → (4x + 3x) + (−2 + 5)
↓
Simplify → 7x + 3
Worked example: Simplify 5a + 2b − 3a + 4b.
(5a − 3a) + (2b + 4b) = 2a + 6b
Exam hint: 2x + 3x = 5x, but 2x + 3x² cannot be combined because the powers differ.

2. Multiplying and dividing algebraic expressions

Use the distributive law: multiply the outside term by every term inside the brackets.
3(x + 4)
↓ Multiply each term by 3
3 × x + 3 × 4
↓
3x + 12
Example: Simplify 2(3x − 5) + 4x.
= 6x − 10 + 4x = 10x − 10

Multiplying two brackets

Expand (x + 2)(x + 3).
= x² + 3x + 2x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6

3. Exponents and exponent laws

An exponent tells you how many times a base is multiplied by itself. For example, 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.

Divide same baseaᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿx⁵ ÷ x² = x³
Power of a power(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ(x²)³ = x⁶

(2 × 4)(x³ × x²) = 8x⁵
When multiplying powers with the same base, add exponents. When dividing, subtract them. For a power raised to another power, multiply the exponents.

4. Surds and square roots

A surd is an irrational root left in radical form, such as √2 or √5.
√72
↓
√(36 × 2)
↓
√36 × √2 = 6√2

Adding and subtracting surds

Simplify 3√2 + 5√2 − √2.
(3 + 5 − 1)√2 = 7√2

Rationalising a denominator

Simplify 1/√3.
(1/√3) × (√3/√3) = √3/3
Do not simplify √2 + √3 to √5. Different surds cannot be combined directly.

5. Factorisation

Factorisation rewrites an expression as a product of simpler factors. Expansion multiplies brackets; factorisation works backwards.

Common factor

6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)

Difference of two squares

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
Factorise x² − 25.
x² − 5² = (x − 5)(x + 5)

Trinomials

Factorise x² + 5x + 6. Find numbers multiplying to 6 and adding to 5: 2 and 3.
x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)

Leading coefficient greater than 1

Factorise 2x² + 7x + 3.
2x² + 6x + x + 3
= 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)
= (2x + 1)(x + 3)
Expand your final brackets to check your factorisation.

6. Linear equations

A linear equation has a highest power of 1 for its unknown. Solve it by keeping both sides balanced.
3x + 5 = 20
↓ Subtract 5 from both sides
3x = 15
↓ Divide by 3
x = 5
↓ Check
3(5) + 5 = 20 ✓

7. Inequalities

Inequalities compare expressions using <, >, ≤ or ≥.
  • x < 4 means x is less than 4.
  • x > 4 means x is greater than 4.
  • x ≤ 4 means x is less than or equal to 4.
  • x ≥ 4 means x is greater than or equal to 4.
Solve 2x + 3 < 11.
2x < 8 → x < 4
Important: multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number reverses its sign.
−2x < 6 → x > −3

8. Quadratic equations

A quadratic equation has a highest power of 2.
ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0
a is the coefficient of x², b is the coefficient of x, and c is the constant.

Solving by factorisation

x² − 5x + 6 = 0
↓
(x − 2)(x − 3) = 0
↓
x = 2 OR x = 3
Zero-product rule: if A × B = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0.

Quadratic formula

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
Solve 2x² − 3x − 2 = 0.
a = 2, b = −3, c = −2
x = (3 ± √(9 + 16)) / 4
x = (3 ± 5) / 4

Answer: x = 2 or x = −½.

9. Nature of roots and the discriminant

The discriminant tells us the nature of the roots.
Δ = b² − 4ac
Δ < 0

No real roots.

Δ = 0

Two equal real roots.

Δ > 0

Two distinct real roots.

For 3x² + 2x − 4 = 0:
Δ = 2² − 4(3)(−4) = 52

Since 52 is positive but not a perfect square, there are two distinct irrational real roots.

x = (−1 ± √13) / 3
The discriminant classifies roots; it does not directly tell you whether each root is positive or negative.

10. Completing the square

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic expression using a squared bracket and a constant.
Rewrite x² + 6x + 5.
x² + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)² − 9 + 5
= (x + 3)² − 4
Take half the coefficient of x, square it, then add and subtract that value.

11. Simultaneous equations

Solve x + y = 10 and x − y = 2.
Add the equations: 2x = 12 → x = 6
6 + y = 10 → y = 4

Answer: x = 6 and y = 4.

Explore number sets used in algebra
  • Natural numbers (ℕ): 1, 2, 3, 4, …
  • Whole numbers (ℕ₀): 0, 1, 2, 3, …
  • Integers (ℤ): …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …
  • Rational numbers (ℚ): numbers expressible as p/q, where q ≠ 0.
  • Irrational numbers: real numbers that cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers.
  • Real numbers (ℝ): all rational and irrational numbers.

Notation for natural numbers may differ between textbooks.

📘 Sequences and Series: Science Clinic

Learn to recognise patterns, calculate the general term, find the position of a term, and calculate sums. Study each worked example before attempting the quiz.

What is a sequence?

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers arranged according to a rule.

What is a series?

A series is the sum of terms in a sequence.

Sequence: 2, 5, 8, 11, …
Series: 2 + 5 + 8 + 11 + …

1. Important symbols

SymbolMeaning
a or T₁First term
TₙThe nth term
nPosition of a term or number of terms
dCommon difference in an arithmetic sequence
rCommon ratio in a geometric sequence
SₙSum of the first n terms
lLast term in a finite sequence

2. How to identify a pattern

Step 1: Write the terms in order.
↓
Step 2: Subtract consecutive terms.
↓
Same difference? It may be arithmetic.
↓
If differences are not the same, check ratios or second differences.
Example A: 4, 7, 10, 13, …

Differences: 7 − 4 = 3, 10 − 7 = 3, 13 − 10 = 3.

The common difference is d = 3, so it is arithmetic.

Example B: 3, 6, 12, 24, …

Ratios: 6 ÷ 3 = 2, 12 ÷ 6 = 2, 24 ÷ 12 = 2.

The common ratio is r = 2, so it is geometric.

3. Arithmetic sequences

An arithmetic sequence has a constant difference between consecutive terms. The difference may be positive, negative, or zero.
Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d
5, 8, 11, 14, …
↓
First term a = 5; common difference d = 3
↓
Tₙ = 5 + (n − 1)3
↓
Tₙ = 3n + 2

Worked example A: Find the 20th term

Sequence: 4, 9, 14, 19, …

Identify the values: a = 4, d = 5, n = 20.

Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d
T₂₀ = 4 + (20 − 1)(5)
T₂₀ = 4 + 95 = 99

Answer: The 20th term is 99.

Worked example B: Find the position of a term

Which term of 7, 11, 15, 19, … is 63?

Here a = 7, d = 4 and Tₙ = 63.

63 = 7 + (n − 1)4
56 = 4(n − 1)
14 = n − 1 → n = 15

Answer: 63 is the 15th term.

4. Arithmetic series: calculating a sum

An arithmetic series is the sum of terms from an arithmetic sequence.
Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d]

If you know the first and last terms, you can use:

Sₙ = n/2 (a + l)
Worked example: Find the sum of the first 10 terms of 2, 5, 8, 11, …

a = 2, d = 3, n = 10.

S₁₀ = 10/2 [2(2) + (10 − 1)3]
= 5[4 + 27] = 155

Answer: The sum is 155.

If the question asks for the sum, use an S formula. If it asks for one particular term, use the T formula.

5. Geometric sequences

A geometric sequence has a constant ratio between consecutive non-zero terms.
Tₙ = arⁿ⁻¹

The common ratio is found by dividing a term by the term immediately before it.

Worked example A: Find the 6th term of 2, 6, 18, 54, …

a = 2, r = 3, n = 6.

T₆ = 2(3)⁵
T₆ = 2 × 243 = 486

Answer: 486.

Worked example B: Find the general term of 80, 40, 20, 10, …

a = 80 and r = 40 ÷ 80 = ½.

Tₙ = 80(½)ⁿ⁻¹

The sequence halves each time.

6. Finite geometric series: calculating a sum

Use the geometric-series formula when adding a fixed number of terms of a geometric sequence.
Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1)/(r − 1), for r ≠ 1

An equivalent form is:

Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r), for r ≠ 1

If r = 1, every term is a, so Sₙ = na.

Worked example: Find the sum of the first four terms of 2, 6, 18, 54, …

a = 2, r = 3 and n = 4.

S₄ = 2(3⁴ − 1)/(3 − 1)
= 2(81 − 1)/2 = 80

Check by adding: 2 + 6 + 18 + 54 = 80.

7. Infinite geometric series

An infinite geometric series continues forever. It has a finite sum only when the common ratio satisfies:
|r| < 1

In that case:

S∞ = a/(1 − r)
Worked example: Find the sum of 12 + 6 + 3 + 1.5 + …

a = 12 and r = ½. Since |½| < 1, the infinite sum exists.

S∞ = 12/(1 − ½) = 12/(½) = 24

Answer: 24.

Important: Do not use the infinite-series formula when |r| ≥ 1. The series does not converge to a finite sum.

8. Quadratic patterns and second differences

A quadratic number pattern often has constant second differences. First, calculate the differences between consecutive terms; then calculate the differences between those differences.
Worked example: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, …
Terms1491625
First differences—3579
Second differences——222

The second differences are constant, so the pattern is quadratic. The general term here is Tₙ = n².

9. Common mistakes and exam tips

  • Do not confuse the common difference d with the common ratio r.
  • In Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d, remember to subtract 1 from n.
  • In Tₙ = arⁿ⁻¹, remember that the exponent is n − 1.
  • Use the term formula for a single term and the sum formula for a series.
  • Use the infinite geometric sum only when |r| < 1.
  • Check that the term number n is a positive whole number when identifying a position in a sequence.
Exam strategy:
  1. Write down the first term.
  2. Calculate the difference or ratio.
  3. Choose the correct formula.
  4. Substitute values carefully using brackets.
  5. Show each calculation step and state the final answer clearly.

Functions and Graphs

A function assigns each allowed input x exactly one output y. Input x → rule → output y.
Quadratic function: y = ax² + bx + c
  • Domain: allowed input values.
  • Range: possible output values.
  • x-intercept: where the graph crosses or touches the x-axis.
  • y-intercept: where the graph crosses the y-axis.
  • Turning point: the maximum or minimum point of a parabola.
To find x-intercepts, set y = 0 and solve. To find the y-intercept, set x = 0.

Finance, Growth and Decay

Compound interest is calculated on the original amount and on interest already accumulated.
A = P(1 + i)ⁿ
  • A = accumulated amount
  • P = initial amount or principal
  • i = interest rate per period as a decimal
  • n = number of periods
R1 000 invested at 10% per year for 2 years: A = 1 000(1.10)² = R1 210.
Convert 8% to 0.08 before substituting. Match the interest rate and number of periods.

Differential Calculus

A derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function and gives the gradient of the tangent.
If y = xⁿ, then dy/dx = nxⁿ⁻¹
Example: y = 3x² + 4x − 1 → dy/dx = 6x + 4.
  • Differentiate powers of x.
  • Find stationary points by setting dy/dx = 0.
  • Use the original function to calculate the y-coordinate.
  • Determine whether a stationary point is a maximum or minimum.
The derivative of a constant is zero.

Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is to occur. 0 means impossible; 1 means certain.
P(A) = favourable outcomes / total equally likely outcomes
For a fair six-sided die, P(rolling a 4) = 1/6.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together.
  • Independent events do not change each other's probabilities.
Check whether outcomes are equally likely before using the simple fraction formula.

Statistics

Statistics involves collecting, organising, analysing and interpreting data.
  • Mean: sum of observations ÷ number of observations.
  • Median: middle observation after arranging data in order.
  • Mode: most frequent value.
  • Range: maximum − minimum.
  • Quartiles: values dividing ordered data into four parts.
  • Standard deviation: measures spread around the mean.
Arrange data in ascending order before finding the median or quartiles.

Analytical Geometry

Analytical geometry uses coordinates and algebra to investigate geometric relationships.
Gradient: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)
Distance: AB = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
Midpoint: M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
Subtract coordinates in the same order in the numerator and denominator when calculating a gradient.

Trigonometry

Trigonometry studies relationships between angles and trigonometric ratios.
sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
tan θ = sin θ / cos θ, when cos θ ≠ 0
sin(A ± B) = sin A cos B ± cos A sin B
Check the specified interval when solving trigonometric equations.

Euclidean geometry uses definitions, established results and theorems to prove relationships between angles, lines and shapes.
