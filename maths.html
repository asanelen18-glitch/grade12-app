What is algebra?

Algebra is the branch of mathematics that uses letters and symbols to represent numbers and relationships.

An unknown number → represent it with x
↓
Describe the relationship → form an equation
↓
Solve the equation → find the unknown value

1. Algebraic expressions and like terms

An algebraic expression is a mathematical phrase containing numbers, variables and operations. It does not have an equals sign unless it is part of an equation.

Example: 3x + 5

Variable → a letter representing a number.
Coefficient → the number multiplying a variable.
Constant → a number without a variable.
Term → a part separated by plus or minus signs.

An expression can contain several terms.

Simplifying like terms

Like terms have the same variables raised to the same powers. Combine their coefficients, but do not combine unlike terms.

4x + 3x − 2 + 5
↓
Group like terms → (4x + 3x) + (−2 + 5)
↓
Simplify → 7x + 3
Worked example: Simplify 5a + 2b − 3a + 4b.

Group the a-terms and the b-terms:

(5a − 3a) + (2b + 4b)
= 2a + 6b
Exam hint: 2x + 3x = 5x, but 2x + 3x² cannot be combined because the powers of x are different.

2. Multiplying and dividing algebraic expressions

When multiplying a number by brackets, use the distributive law: multiply the outside term by every term inside the brackets.
3(x + 4)
↓ Multiply each term by 3
3 × x + 3 × 4
↓
3x + 12
Example: Simplify 2(3x − 5) + 4x.
= 6x − 10 + 4x
= 10x − 10

Multiplying two brackets

Expand (x + 2)(x + 3).
= x(x + 3) + 2(x + 3)
= x² + 3x + 2x + 6
= x² + 5x + 6

An exponent tells you how many times a base is multiplied by itself.

For example: 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.

4. Surds and square roots

A surd is an irrational root that is left in radical form, such as √2 or √5.

Simplifying a surd means removing any perfect-square factor from inside the square root.

√72
↓ Find a perfect-square factor
√(36 × 2)
↓ Separate the square roots
√36 × √2
↓
6√2

Adding and subtracting surds

Simplify 3√2 + 5√2 − √2.
(3 + 5 − 1)√2 = 7√2

Only like surds can be combined directly.

Rationalising a denominator

Rationalising means removing a surd from the denominator of a fraction.

Simplify 1/√3.
1/√3 × √3/√3
= √3/3
Do not add the numbers inside different surds: √2 + √3 cannot be simplified to √5.

5. Factorisation

Factorisation means rewriting an expression as a product of simpler factors.

Expansion → multiply brackets. Factorisation → work backwards to brackets.

Method A: Common factor

6x² + 9x
↓ Find the common factor: 3x
3x(2x + 3)

Method B: Difference of two squares

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
Factorise x² − 25.
= x² − 5²
= (x − 5)(x + 5)

Method C: Trinomials

Factorise x² + 5x + 6.

Find two numbers that:

  • Multiply to give 6.
  • Add to give 5.

The numbers are 2 and 3.

x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)

Method D: Quadratic trinomials with a leading coefficient

Factorise 2x² + 7x + 3.

Multiply the first and last coefficients: 2 × 3 = 6.

Find two numbers whose product is 6 and sum is 7: 6 and 1.

2x² + 6x + x + 3
= 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)
= (2x + 1)(x + 3)
Factorisation check: expand your final brackets. If the original expression returns, your factorisation is correct.

6. Linear equations

A linear equation has a highest power of 1 for its unknown variable.

Solving an equation means finding the value that makes both sides equal.

3x + 5 = 20
↓ Subtract 5 from both sides
3x = 15
↓ Divide both sides by 3
x = 5
↓ Check: 3(5) + 5 = 20
Correct ✓
Whatever operation you perform on one side of an equation, perform the same operation on the other side.

7. Inequalities

An inequality compares two expressions using symbols instead of an equals sign.
  • x < 4 means x is less than 4.
  • x > 4 means x is greater than 4.
  • x ≤ 4 means x is less than or equal to 4.
  • x ≥ 4 means x is greater than or equal to 4.
Solve 2x + 3 < 11.
2x < 8
x < 4
Important rule: when multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign.
−2x < 6 → x > −3

8. Quadratic equations

A quadratic equation is an equation whose highest power of the unknown is 2.
ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0

a → coefficient of x²
b → coefficient of x
c → constant term

Method A: Solve by factorisation

x² − 5x + 6 = 0
↓ Factorise
(x − 2)(x − 3) = 0
↓ Set each factor equal to zero
x − 2 = 0 OR x − 3 = 0
↓
x = 2 OR x = 3
Zero-product rule: if A × B = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0. This rule applies after the equation has been written as a product equal to zero.

Method B: Quadratic formula

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

Then substitute carefully into the quadratic formula.

Worked example: Solve 2x² − 3x − 2 = 0.

Step 1: Identify the coefficients.

a = 2, b = −3, c = −2

Step 2: Substitute into the formula.

x = (−(−3) ± √[(-3)² − 4(2)(−2)]) / 4

Step 3: Simplify.

x = (3 ± √(9 + 16)) / 4
x = (3 ± 5) / 4

Step 4: Calculate both roots.

x = 2 OR x = −½

9. Nature of roots and the discriminant

The discriminant is the expression under the square root in the quadratic formula. It tells us about the roots.
Δ = b² − 4ac
Calculate Δ = b² − 4ac
↓
Δ < 0

No real roots.

Δ = 0

Two equal real roots (a repeated root).

Δ > 0

Two distinct real roots.

For integer coefficients, a perfect-square discriminant gives rational roots; a non-square discriminant gives irrational roots.

Visualising roots on a parabola

Illustration only; the actual shape and intercepts depend on the equation.

Worked example: Determine the nature of the roots of 3x² + 2x − 4 = 0.

Identify: a = 3, b = 2, c = −4.

Δ = b² − 4ac
= 2² − 4(3)(−4)
= 4 + 48
= 52

52 is positive → two distinct real roots. 52 is not a perfect square → the roots are irrational.

Solving fully:

x = (−2 ± √52) / 6
x = (−1 ± √13) / 3
Do not confuse the sign of the discriminant with the signs of the roots. The discriminant classifies the roots; it does not directly tell you whether each root is positive or negative.

10. Completing the square

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic expression using a squared bracket and a constant.
Rewrite x² + 6x + 5 by completing the square.

Step 1: Take half of the coefficient of x: 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

Step 2: Square that number: 3² = 9.

x² + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)² − 9 + 5
= (x + 3)² − 4
For x² + bx, half b, then square it. Add and subtract the same value so that the expression remains unchanged.

11. Simultaneous equations

Simultaneous equations are two or more equations solved together to find values that satisfy all of them.
Solve:
x + y = 10
x − y = 2

Add the equations so that y cancels:

2x = 12 → x = 6

Substitute x = 6 into x + y = 10:

6 + y = 10 → y = 4

Answer: x = 6 and y = 4.

Explore number sets used in algebra
  • Natural numbers (ℕ): 1, 2, 3, 4, …
  • Whole numbers (ℕ₀): 0, 1, 2, 3, …
  • Integers (ℤ): …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …
  • Rational numbers (ℚ): numbers expressible as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • Irrational numbers: real numbers that cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers, such as √2.
  • Real numbers (ℝ): all rational and irrational numbers.
  • Non-real complex numbers: numbers involving i, where i² = −1.

Notation for natural numbers may differ between textbooks.

Sequences and Series

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers following a pattern → a series is the sum of the terms in a sequence.

Arithmetic sequence

Each term is found by adding the same common difference, d.

Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d

Geometric sequence

Each term is found by multiplying by the same common ratio, r.

Tₙ = arⁿ⁻¹
Hint: subtract consecutive terms to check for an arithmetic pattern. Divide consecutive non-zero terms to check for a geometric pattern.
Example: 3, 7, 11, 15, … → common difference d = 4 → Tₙ = 3 + 4(n − 1).

Functions and Graphs

A function assigns each allowed input x exactly one output y. Input x → rule → output y.
Quadratic function: y = ax² + bx + c
  • Domain: the allowed input values.
  • Range: the possible output values.
  • x-intercept: where the graph crosses or touches the x-axis.
  • y-intercept: where the graph crosses the y-axis.
  • Turning point: the maximum or minimum point of a parabola.
Hint: to find x-intercepts, set y = 0 and solve. To find the y-intercept, set x = 0.

Finance, Growth and Decay

Compound interest is interest calculated on the original amount and on interest already accumulated → the balance changes each period.
A = P(1 + i)ⁿ
  • A = accumulated amount
  • P = initial amount or principal
  • i = interest rate per period, written as a decimal
  • n = number of periods
Example: R1 000 invested at 10% per year for 2 years: A = 1 000(1.10)² = R1 210.
Hint: convert 8% to 0.08 before substituting. Make sure the interest rate and number of periods use the same time unit.

Differential Calculus

A derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function → geometrically, it gives the gradient of the tangent.
If y = xⁿ, then dy/dx = nxⁿ⁻¹
Example: y = 3x² + 4x − 1
Differentiate each term → dy/dx = 6x + 4.
  • Differentiate powers of x.
  • Find stationary points by setting dy/dx = 0.
  • Use the original function to calculate the y-coordinate.
  • Determine whether a stationary point is a maximum or minimum.
Hint: the derivative of a constant is zero.

Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is to occur. 0 → impossible event; 1 → certain event.
P(A) = favourable outcomes / total equally likely outcomes
Example: a fair six-sided die → P(rolling a 4) = 1/6.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together.
  • Independent events do not change each other's probabilities.
Hint: check whether the outcomes are equally likely before using the simple fraction formula.

Statistics

Statistics is the collection, organisation, analysis and interpretation of data → use numerical summaries and graphs to describe a data set.
  • Mean: sum of observations ÷ number of observations.
  • Median: middle observation after arranging data in order.
  • Mode: most frequently occurring value.
  • Range: maximum − minimum.
  • Quartiles: values that divide ordered data into four parts.
  • Standard deviation: measures the spread of data around its mean.
Hint: arrange the data in ascending order before finding the median or quartiles.

Analytical Geometry

Analytical geometry uses coordinates and algebra to investigate geometric relationships → points on a plane can be described using (x, y).
Gradient: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)
Distance: AB = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
Midpoint: M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
Hint: subtract coordinates in the same order in the numerator and denominator when calculating a gradient.

Trigonometry

Trigonometry studies relationships between angles and trigonometric ratios → identities help simplify expressions and solve equations.
sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
tan θ = sin θ / cos θ, when cos θ ≠ 0
sin(A ± B) = sin A cos B ± cos A sin B
Hint: check the specified interval when solving trigonometric equations.

Euclidean Geometry

Euclidean geometry uses definitions, established results and theorems to prove relationships between angles, lines and shapes.
